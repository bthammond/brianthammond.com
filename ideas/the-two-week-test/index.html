The two-week test

Don’t ask whether your business could run without you. Watch what it actually does.

Brian T. Hammond · October 2026 · 4 min read

Most owners can tell you how their business is supposed to run. Fewer can tell you how it actually runs when they’re not there. Those are different questions, and only the second one matters.

So here’s a test. Imagine it’s Monday and you’re somewhere with no signal for two weeks. Nothing is on fire. You’re simply not reachable. Now walk through the fortnight honestly. Not what’s supposed to happen. What would actually happen.

Walk through the two weeks

  • Monday morning. A customer asks for pricing outside the standard sheet. Does someone handle it, or does it sit until you’re back?
  • Wednesday. A supplier wants an answer on a price increase by Friday. Who decides?
  • Thursday. A manager needs to spend four thousand dollars today to fix a problem. Do they know their limit, or do they call you?
  • End of week one. Your largest customer calls with a problem. Do they ask for their account lead, or for you?
  • Week two. Two of your managers disagree about something that matters. Do they settle it, or wait for you?

You don’t need to take the trip to learn the answers. You need fifteen honest minutes.

Why behavior, not intentions

Your org chart says who is responsible. Your job descriptions say who is accountable. Neither tells you who actually decides. Behavior does: who picks up the phone, who approves the exception, who people wait for. When the chart and the behavior disagree, the behavior is the truth.

That’s why I start there. You can redraw an org chart in an afternoon and change nothing. Change what people actually do on an ordinary Wednesday, and the results follow.

What the test usually shows

The bottleneck is rarely one big thing. It’s a dozen small habits. Answering the text. Approving the exception. Taking the call because it’s faster. Each one is reasonable on its own. Together, they train a company to wait for you.

And it isn’t anyone’s fault. At the start, that was the only way the business could work. It just never got redesigned.

What to do with what you find

  1. Pick one moment where the honest answer was “it waits for me.” Just one.
  2. Name the person who should own it. A person, not “the team.”
  3. Write down the limit they can act within: dollars, scope, or both.
  4. Tell them out loud that it’s theirs now.
  5. Send it back. When the question comes to you anyway, and it will, hand it back to them. That’s the hard part, and the part that changes the behavior.

The first few times will feel slower. That’s the cost of changing a habit, and it’s temporary. Then pick the next moment.
